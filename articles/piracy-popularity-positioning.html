Piracy, popularity, and positioning
2022 · Written for DEDS, MSc ESB, Imperial College London

"Any publicity is good publicity."

Introduction

The protagonist here is the Korean pop group BTS, who are managed by HYBE Labels. This paper seeks to examine the effects of illegal consumption and distribution of paid content featuring the band (such as documentaries, movies, or reality series), the trade-off HYBE faces while countering the problem, and further steps HYBE can take in this situation.

Context

Korean media has gained immense popularity over the past few years, mainly led by the global success of BTS. The most important reason for this has been the strength of the fanbase — known as ARMY. K-pop fan culture is different from Western pop fan culture: artists are known as idols, and every group has a specific name for their fans. K-pop fan culture is driven by the strength of the community, characterised by fans' dedication to and participation in idols' success. The way companies make money is by capitalising on the relationship between the fans and the idols.

The Problem

Throughout the years, HYBE has released a variety of content featuring BTS on a Usage Based Pricing model — you pay for every piece of content you want to watch, rather than a single fee for the full catalogue. However, there are dedicated webpages that stream and distribute this paid content for free, as well as YouTube and Dailymotion channels doing the same.

The following equation contextualises why it is easier to look for content illegally than to buy it, especially when viewers are in the sampling phase of the lock-in cycle:

Price to pay for the content > search costs + probability of getting caught × fine + shame associated with consuming content illegally

The most popular site sharing paid content for free, rubberdeokies, has 843.6K Twitter followers. Assuming 75% of consumers don't subscribe, that is ~3.37M people. At an average content spend of 9 per user, this translates to a total loss in sales revenue of ~32M over five years — against HYBE's total sales revenue of .4B over the same period.

The Trade-off

The reason HYBE isn't simply shutting down piracy sites is a genuine trade-off:

  • K-pop is centred around the artist–fan relationship, and BTS have first-mover advantage in capitalising on this.
  • The deeper the bond between fan and idol, the higher the following — which means higher sales of licensed goods, merchandise, and brand partnerships (Samsung, Hyundai, Louis Vuitton, among others).
  • When more content is accessible, it pushes viewers faster from the sampling to the entrenchment stage of the lock-in cycle, developing higher affection and benefit from following the artists.
  • On entering entrenchment, the shame of consuming illegal content rises and the pride of fandom membership tips the scales — the equation reverses.
  • The lock-in phase is when the viewer becomes an ARMY — willing to spend on albums, merchandise, paid content, and concert tickets.

If HYBE shuts down these sites, viewers lose the content that accelerates their transition from viewer to fan.

What Should HYBE Do?

In my opinion, HYBE should allow these sites to be. The conversion of viewers to fans opens up more revenue streams. There is also an implicit network effect: joining a fandom and playing an active role makes people feel significant as part of a group, so more fans create more value for existing fans ("I was here first"). The value of the ARMY network increases as more people become ARMYs.

Fans also support fellow idol groups from the same label — a spill-over effect that increases revenue as HYBE taps into existing fanbases for new groups. In Q3 2022 alone, HYBE's revenue was 58M, composed of album sales, concerts, ads, appearances, merchandising, licensing, contents, and fan club subscriptions.

HYBE also uses Dyson's Dictum: physical albums are packed with additional perks (such as guaranteed entry into fansign raffles), boosting physical sales and maintaining steady revenue for months following a release. Merchandise kits are bundled with perks like membership IDs, pre-recording access, and early concert ticket access — reducing dispersion in willingness to pay.

At this point, BTS has generated high-margin profits for HYBE and built a large fandom. Spending money on sophisticated encryption and legal enforcement would be a futile exercise — the fandom would find another way to access the content. Since the benefits clearly outweigh the costs, it is better for HYBE to let the sites be.
