X-inefficiency in Sports Pt. 1
February 2021

A few days ago our resident Man U president, Ashish Kulkarni, wrote a wonderful blog post about X-inefficiency. I clicked on it because there's at least one efficiency in economics I need to know about — so naturally, I read up about it.

X-inefficiency as a concept arose in this paper by Harvey Leibenstein. When there is no major incentive to control costs (as in the real world, profit maximisation does not always imply cost minimization), the firm ends up producing at a cost more than what it can actually achieve. The firm is not technically efficient — it is thus, x-inefficient. Sports teams can take the place of firms here. A professional sports league is a monopoly due to the very high costs of entry, and the teams are the firms. Monopolies are perfect examples of x-inefficiencies: due to lack of competitive pressure, there is no real incentive for firms to cut down on costs and produce exactly on the average cost curve.

The first paper we look at is by Manuel Espitia-Escuer and Lucía Isabel García Cebrián, examining the efficiency of football teams in the Champions League between 2003 and 2007.

  • 2003 (Old Trafford): Winner A.C. Milan beat Juventus on penalties in the first all-Italian UCL final.
  • 2004 (Arena AufSchalke): Winner FC Porto beat Monaco.
  • 2005 (Atatürk Olympic Stadium, Turkey): Winner Liverpool FC beat A.C. Milan. The trophy was permanently awarded to Liverpool and a new trophy made for subsequent seasons.
  • 2006 (Stade de France): Winner FC Barcelona beat Arsenal. Liverpool failed to qualify but were given special dispensation by UEFA; they were eliminated by Benfica in the first knockout round.
  • 2007 (Olympic Stadium, Athens): Winner A.C. Milan beat Liverpool. Milan players swept the UEFA Club Football Player Awards, with Kaká winning two honours.

The paper, in brief: statistics show that efficiency is very important and the more efficient you are, the higher are your chances of winning. Measuring efficiency allows a team to better interpret and evaluate their results and thus allows for better improvements. The inefficiencies observed could be a result of wasteful use of resources rather than differences in tactics. And while efficiency is relative to other teams in a single season, no team is consistently efficient throughout the full period of analysis.

In Part 2, we will look at this study in more detail along with a few more.
