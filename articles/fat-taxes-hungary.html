How does economics underpin the design and implementation of public policies — the case of 'fat' taxes in Hungary
2022 · Written for EPP, MSc ESB, Imperial College London

Introduction

The fat tax was introduced to address the issue of obesity and was levied on 'unhealthy' products in the Hungarian food market. The main aim was to reduce the consumption of fatty, processed foods that were causing adverse health effects and negative knock-on effects on consumer lifestyles. The government waited for the market to take corrective measures — but when those didn't materialise, it intervened.

How the policy aligns with economic theory

Initially the policy was ex-ante — designed to impact future events. Subsequent reforms were carried out to account for shortfalls, making it ex-post in practice.

The tax was a Pigouvian tax — a flat rate introduced to bring private costs up to the level of social costs being borne by consumers, negating the negative externalities caused by the consumption of unhealthy goods and the subsequent decrease in health outcomes.

The tax also increased competition by pushing for innovation in low-cost and healthier alternatives. As universal healthcare is guaranteed in Hungary, the tax helped solve the problem of information asymmetry: increased prices signal to consumers that a product is unhealthy, helping correct for morally hazardous behaviour. This also acted as a nudge — pushing people towards behavioural reform by incentivising healthier choices and helping combat the circularity of asymmetric information in the food market.

The taxes accounted for both abatement and adaptation costs. Abatement costs are the costs incurred to stop the impact of a negative consequence; adaptation costs account for living with that impact. Here, the taxes were abating consumption of unhealthy products and adapting to the knock-on effects of previous unhealthy consumption by adding tax revenue to Hungary's healthcare system — providing an additional funding source to treat consumers with newer and more advanced resources.

Where the policy does not align with economic theory

There were areas where the design and implementation did not align with economic principles.

Due to decreased profits from taxed revenue, small and local businesses without high profit margins were heavily affected, pushing some out of the market. This reduced market competitiveness and enabled monopoly power to continue. Healthier alternatives are often more expensive due to lower economies of scale, which may also increase input costs for small businesses.

As shown in Biro (2021), the tax mostly affected the lowest-income households in the country. This brings in the classic trade-off between equity and efficiency that governments always face — in this case, equity was the casualty, constituting a government failure.

Another government failure was that the tax encouraged consumers to shift from higher-priced supermarkets to discount stores and lower-quality food-grade items. Once consumers are habituated to certain products — especially those containing ingredients that cause symptoms akin to addictive substances, such as high sugar or processed food content — it becomes a regular part of their lifestyle. This may lead to adverse selection as consumers compensate for higher prices by seeking cheaper, lower-quality versions of the same products.

Furthermore, there are no conclusive studies with a high certainty score showing significant improvement in health outcomes attributable to the introduction of this fat tax.

Conclusion

While most parts of the policy design and implementation were in line with economic theory, there is still insufficient evidence to conclusively prove its real-world impact. There are also adverse effects that have emerged from its implementation. The fat tax is a useful case study in both the promise and the limits of Pigouvian taxation as a public health instrument.
