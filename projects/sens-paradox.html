Sen's Paradox in Lean 4
Completed · May 2026  ·  View on GitHub →

Introduction

Amartya Sen published Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal in 1970, marking a landmark contribution to welfare economics. For his seminal contributions to the field, he won the Nobel Prize for Economics in 1998. The paper formalises the concept of individual liberty in an extremely weak form and examines its consequences for broader society, borrowing logically from Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1951).

This project formalises the entire paper in Lean 4, writing out the logic of Sen's proof in a form that a theorem prover can automatically verify.

Setup: States, Individuals, and Social Preferences

Sen defines Ri as the ordering of the ith individual over the set X of all possible social states, across n individuals. R is the social preference relation to be determined. He then defines three types of collective choice rules:

  • Collective Choice Rule (CCR): A functional relation f such that for any set of n individual orderings, exactly one social preference relation is determined.
  • Social Welfare Function (SWF): A CCR restricted to orderings. SWF ⊂ SDF ⊂ CCR.
  • Social Decision Function (SDF): A CCR restricted to social preference relations that generate a choice function — the "best" element from any subset of X.

The Four Conditions

Condition U (Unrestricted Domain): Every logically possible set of individual orderings is in the domain of the CCR.
Condition P (Pareto Principle): If every individual prefers x to y, society must prefer x to y. Formally: xPiy ∀ i ∈ N ⟹ xPy.
Condition L (Liberalism): For each individual i, there is at least one pair of alternatives (x, y) such that if the individual prefers x to y, society's preference follows.
Condition L' (Weak Liberalism): A weaker version — only two individuals k and j are decisive over their respective pairs (x,y) and (z,w). ∃ at least two individuals (i, j) ∈ N; ∃ (x,y), (z,w) s.t. xPiy ⟹ xPy and zPjw ⟹ zPw.

The Theorems

Theorem I: There is no SDF that can simultaneously satisfy Conditions U, P, and L.

Theorem II: There is no SDF that can simultaneously satisfy Conditions U, P, and L'. (Theorem I follows as a corollary, since L' is weaker than L.)

Proof of Theorem II

Two individuals (1 and 2) and two choice sets: Individual 1 over (x, y); Individual 2 over (z, w). Two cases are considered:

Case a) x = z (one alternative in common): Everyone prefers y to w. By Condition L': xPy and wPx. By Condition P: yPw. This creates a cycle x → y → w → x with no best element, contradicting the existence of a choice function. No SDF exists.

Case b) All four alternatives distinct: Individual 1 prefers x to y, w to z, and (by Pareto) y to z. Individual 2 prefers z to w, y to z, and (by Pareto) w to x. Condition L' gives xPy and zPw; Condition P gives wPx and yPz. Again, no best alternative exists in this choice set — no SDF exists.
